OpenObserve · Metrics Benchmark

One Dataset, Four Systems — Benchmarking Prometheus, Mimir, and OpenObserve on Metrics Ingestion and Queries

The problem with most metrics-system benchmarks is that everyone tests their own way: different data, different hardware, different queries. The results can't be compared.

So we pushed the same ~1.2 billion samples through one OTel Collector into four systems simultaneously, stopped ingestion, and ran the exact same PromQL against each over an identical frozen window.

The contenders are four deployments with fully aligned configurations: Prometheus, Grafana Mimir, OpenObserve (Parquet format), and OpenObserve (Vortex format). Each system gets a dedicated EC2 m7gd.2xlarge with its data on the node's local NVMe, query caches disabled, the same pinned time range, and identical query limits and timeouts. Ingestion is stopped before measuring, so all four query a frozen, identical dataset. The whole benchmark was then run twice — at a 28G and a 14G container limit — because those answer different questions: how fast, and what still runs at all.

The setup

Pin down the variables first — the numbers only mean something afterward.

  • Hardware: one dedicated EC2 m7gd.2xlarge per system, 7 CPU, 474 GB local NVMe; container limit 28G in round 1 and 14G in round 2
  • Versions: Prometheus quay.io/prometheus/prometheus:v3.6.0; Mimir grafana/mimir:latest; OpenObserve 0.92.0-rc3, two OpenObserve deployments as an A/B: one with ZO_FILE_FORMAT=parquet, one with ZO_FILE_FORMAT=vortex. All single-node
  • Fairness settings: all query caches disabled; OpenObserve's Parquet filter pushdown disabled (ZO_FEATURE_PUSHDOWN_FILTER_ENABLED=false — it's on by default, but in our metrics tests it actually slowed queries by ~20%, so we turned it off; the switch only affects Parquet, Vortex's filter pushdown is always on)
  • Query range: 2026-08-06 00:00 – 03:00 (CST), every query executed 3 times, all three results shown in the tables

How the data was generated

The workload comes from openobserve/fake-webserver:v2: 24 pods, each producing ~45k time series, so the histogram's _bucket metric carries roughly 1.09 million active series.

On the collection side, a separately deployed OTel Collector writes the same data to all systems under test simultaneously — every system receives the exact same samples, ~1.2 billion across the codelab_api_* metrics, spanning 1,085,760 bucket series.

Cardinality of the two key metrics:

  • codelab_api_request_duration_seconds_bucket: 1,085,760 series (high cardinality; used by the histogram queries)
  • codelab_api_request_duration_seconds_count: 41,760 series (used by the irate query)

Keep that million-series bucket cardinality in mind — it's the root cause of Prometheus and Mimir falling over on the unfiltered histogram later.

Ingestion: resource usage

Measured over a 3-hour steady-state ingestion window (2026-08-07 07:00–10:00 UTC), from the container metrics reported per pod:

SystemCPU (typical)CPU (peak)MemoryDisk
Prometheus1.252.32.8 → 4.3 GB, rising4.5 GB
Mimir0.92.24.0 → 5.2 GB, rising7.4 GB
OpenObserve (Parquet)2.43.01.2 → 1.5 GB, flat15.5 GB
OpenObserve (Vortex)2.03.81.4 → 1.8 GB, flat15.1 GB

Three observations:

Memory is OpenObserve's clear win. Sampled every ten minutes across 8h23m of ingestion, Prometheus ran 3.2–4.1GB and Mimir 4.5–5.5GB, both moving in a sawtooth as head blocks fill and compact. Both OpenObserve variants stay flat at 1.5–2.1GB — roughly half of Prometheus and a third of Mimir. Once ingestion stops everything collapses: Prometheus to 2.4GB, Mimir to 2.5GB, and both OpenObserve deployments to under 600MB; the ingestion-time figures are buffers and WAL, not resident working set.

OpenObserve pays for it in CPU. ~2.0–2.4 cores versus Prometheus's ~1.25 and Mimir's ~0.9 — roughly 2× and 2.5×. The extra cycles go into columnar encoding and compression. At identical ingest throughput, that is the price paid for query performance and low memory.

A note on measuring memory. These figures are RSS (k8s.pod.memory.rss, anonymous pages only). Which metric you pick decides the answer: by RSS, OpenObserve holds 1.5GB against Prometheus's 4.3GB; by cgroup memory.workingSetBytes it reads ~10GB against ~3.5GB — the ordering inverts. Working set is RSS plus active page cache charged to the cgroup, and a system continuously writing columnar files accumulates a lot of that: cache the kernel reclaims on demand, not memory the process needs. RSS is not perfect either — it excludes file-backed mmap pages, so it undercounts Prometheus and Mimir, which mmap their chunk files — but they still come out well above OpenObserve. Whichever you use, say which.

Disk usage is largest for OpenObserve, and it deserves context. 29GB versus Prometheus's 11GB, over 8h23m of ingestion. A general-purpose columnar format keeps full detail rather than applying TSDB-style XOR/delta compression tuned specifically for time series; in exchange, the same data simultaneously serves SQL, PromQL, and arbitrary dimensional analysis, and maps naturally onto object storage, where it typically lives in production at a very different unit cost. Parquet and Vortex tie, at 29 and 28GB. Mimir's 28GB is provisional — measured ~2 hours after the last write, while its compactor was still holding superseded source blocks for deletion_delay: 12h. Its volume read 21.6GB at the moment of stop and rose to 29.5GB during compaction; it does not settle until ~14 hours after ingestion ends.

Disk is the one number you cannot measure right after stopping ingestion. Watched live across this run, Mimir's volume went up after the writes stopped — 21.6GB at 21:10, 29.5GB by 22:23 — because the compactor writes the merged block first and keeps the sources for half a day. Two stable readings 30 seconds apart prove nothing.

The wait is not symmetric. OpenObserve deletes merged-away files after 2h (ZO_COMPACT_DELETE_FILES_DELAY_HOURS) and settles at ~2h; Prometheus deletes source blocks immediately after compaction and settles at ~2–3h; Mimir needs ~14h, and neither of its timers can usefully be shortened. Its PVC holds two copies: the filesystem bucket, whose superseded source blocks the compactor keeps for deletion_delay: 12h, and the ingester's local TSDB, kept for blocks_storage.tsdb.retention_period: 13h after upload. Lowering the first only moves an intermediate drop earlier — the volume still waits on the 13-hour timer — and the second must not be lowered at all: it is aligned with query_store_after: 12h, and dropping the ingester's copy early makes queries silently return incomplete results. Wait ~14 hours, or say your disk figures are provisional.

Measuring early biases the comparison towards OpenObserve, by inflating the systems it is compared against. Wait ~14 hours after the last write, or label your disk figures provisional — as the table above does for Mimir.

Queries: four PromQL expressions, three time windows

The query tests cover the four most common dashboard workloads — irate, unfiltered histogram, regex-filtered histogram, equality-filtered histogram — each run over 30-minute, 1-hour, and 3-hour windows. Tables show the raw latency of all three runs, in ms.

The two OpenObserve variants run neck and neck — and lead throughout. On the 3-hour window, Parquet's median of 409ms is about 8.4× faster than Prometheus (3,450ms) and about 21× faster than Mimir (8,700ms). The growth curve matters even more: stretching the window from 30m to 3h (6× the data), Prometheus latency grows 4.8×, Mimir 10.9×, while OpenObserve grows only 5× and stays under half a second in absolute terms. Drag a dashboard's time range wider and the perceived gap keeps growing.

Against a million-series unfiltered histogram, the defaults decide the outcome before the engines do: Prometheus and Mimir both refuse the query at stock limits. Given the same allowances and the same 600s clock, all four finish every window — and the spread is an order of magnitude.

At stock settings Prometheus and Mimir refuse this query rather than run it — Prometheus with query processing would load too many samples into memory, Mimir with err-mimir-max-chunks-per-query. Both are protective limits, and this benchmark raises them (--query.max-samples=1e9, max_fetched_chunks_per_query=20e6) together with a uniform 600s timeout, so the engines decide the outcome instead of the defaults. All four then complete every window — and OpenObserve finishes the 3h scan in 27 seconds against Prometheus's 3.3 minutes and Mimir's 4.3 minutes.

Parquet vs Vortex: same engine, two file formats

The only difference between the two OpenObserve deployments is ZO_FILE_FORMAT. The results split cleanly by query type:

irate and unfiltered histogram: a tie. When queries must scan all the data, the two formats trade places within 5–10% — no decisive winner. Disk usage ties as well (15.5 and 15.1GB).

Filtered histogram: Vortex is consistently 2× faster or more.

The pattern is remarkably stable and widens with the window: 2.9× at 30m, 3.0× at 1h, 3.3× at 3h and 3.3× at 6h. At the 6-hour window Parquet is slower than both Prometheus and Mimir on this query while Vortex is roughly twice as fast as either — for dashboard-style filtered workloads the format choice is worth more than the engine choice.

Put differently: the "filter by service/endpoint" queries that dominate real dashboards are exactly the shape where Vortex gains the most.

Round 2: the same benchmark at 14G

Beyond the headline numbers, one phenomenon is worth recording.

Halving the memory changes almost nothing — except that Prometheus stops being able to answer. The entire benchmark was re-run at a 14G limit against the same frozen dataset. Every cell landed within 5% of its 28G value, with two exceptions: the million-series unfiltered histogram at 3h and at 6h, where Prometheus was OOMKilled both times, mid-query, after 158s and 192s respectively (restartCount=2, exitCode=137).

The reason shows up in a direct measurement. A single 3-hour unfiltered histogram peaks at 18.7GB RSS in Prometheus against 4.25GB in Mimir — Prometheus loads samples into memory, and at --query.max-samples=1e9 and ~16 bytes per sample that is ~16GB, matching the measured rise almost exactly. Raising the limit so the query can run, and having the query consume 20GB, are the same decision. Mimir answers it in a quarter of the memory and takes 35% longer; on a 14G box that is the difference between an answer and a restart.

Worth noting how this was captured at all: curl cannot distinguish an OOMKill from a network failure — both surface as HTTP 000. The harness records a per-request timestamp and samples container restart state before and after each run, so finishedAt=16:58:26Z could be matched to the exact request that triggered it. Without that, this result would read "connection failure" and mean nothing.

The bottleneck is disk throughput — the arithmetic makes it obvious. The query engine log shows:

promql->search->storage: load files 76,
scan_size 137424238093, compressed_size 3627283262, took: 3 ms

Locating the 76 files via metadata took just 3ms, but the compressed data to read off disk is 3.6GB. The test used gp3 at its default configuration — 3,000 IOPS, 125MB/s throughput: 3.6GB ÷ 125MB/s ≈ 29 seconds, which lines up almost exactly with the measured 30 seconds. Cold query time is spent almost entirely reading data off the disk.

We verified the diagnosis: after switching gp3 to io2, the same cold query takes only about 4 seconds (3.52s measured) — barely distinguishable from a hot run. The cold-query bottleneck is purely storage bandwidth; a faster disk or an added cache layer buys an order-of-magnitude improvement.

The bottom line

Put the same ~1.2 billion samples and ~1.09 million bucket series in front of four systems, and the results condense to four sentences:

  • Ingestion memory: OpenObserve stays flat at 1.5–2.1GB — about half of Prometheus and a third of Mimir, and under 600MB once ingestion stops. The trade-off is ~2.6× the disk of Prometheus (typically on object storage)
  • Everyday queries: on irate at 6h, OpenObserve is ~11.2× faster than Prometheus and ~9.0× faster than Mimir; on filtered histograms Vortex leads Prometheus by 2.0×, while Parquet trails it by 1.7×
  • The high-cardinality limit: on the million-series unfiltered histogram, stock limits make Prometheus and Mimir refuse the query outright. Raised to match, all four complete — OpenObserve in 27s at 3h against 3.3 and 4.3 minutes
  • The format race: Vortex is 2.9×–3.3× faster than Parquet on filtered queries and ties on full scans — for dashboard-style filtered workloads, Vortex is the better default
In high-cardinality metrics, the real dividing line isn't a few hundred milliseconds of latency — it's whether your queries still return at all once series counts cross a million.
